# Research notes: cargo-ship reference videos, Sea of Thieves ocean, JeffHK route

## Method and limits

- Video titles, authors, and description text below were recovered from YouTube index/oEmbed
  metadata and from contemporaneous pages that quote the descriptions verbatim. YouTube watch
  pages do not expose full description text to a plain fetch, so quoted descriptions are cited
  to the mirror/index pages that reproduce them, in addition to the canonical watch URL.
- No timestamp is given unless it appears in a sourced description or a sourced page quoting
  it. Where a video has no chapter list in any sourced description found, that is stated
  explicitly instead of inventing beats with times.
- "What the viewer sees" is therefore a description-based reconstruction (camera position,
  subject, weather/traffic named in the description or in press coverage), not a frame-by-frame
  viewing log. Interpretive/production takeaways are labelled as synthesis.
- Route coordinates in §3 are representative shipping-lane / port coordinates, not a recovered
  AIS track. JeffHK's description names only the legs; it does not publish coordinates.

### 1.4 Cross-video visual grammar (synthesis for recreation)

- One locked, high, wide POV per video; the ship's own bow/containers/bridge structures are
  the constant foreground and the only reliable scale cue. Horizon sits high or mid-frame;
  sky occupies a very large share of the frame, so sky/cloud/star rendering carries as much
  weight as the ocean shader.
- Three different time treatments of the same subject: JeffHK compresses 30 days into
  ~10 minutes (weather, ports, and traffic become events; individual waves vanish), while
  both Boyle videos are real-time and uncut (wave period, ship motion, and arrival pacing
  are truthful; "events" are slow builds and landmark passes).
- Three different waters: exposed North Atlantic swell building over hours (§1.1), sheltered
  channel/harbour water on arrival (§1.2), and mostly tropical open-ocean + strait water
  whose surface is secondary to sky and traffic at timelapse speed (§1.3).
- Recurring beats any recreation should be able to stage: clear night sky / Milky Way over
  a dark sea; sunrise/sunset colour ramps reflected on water; a rain/lightning squall with
  a wet lens; port cargo operation as a burst of small fast motions; fishing-boat lights at
  night; a final congested-traffic arrival with land closing in.

## 2. Sea of Thieves ocean technique, and what transfers to a WebGPU ocean

### 2.1 Which talk actually documents the ocean (correction to "GDC talks")

  Talk PDF: https://history.siggraph.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/2018-Talks-Ang_The-Technical-Art-of-Sea-of-Thieves.pdf
  Recorded talk (YouTube upload cited by later work): https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=y9BOz2dFZzs

### 2.2 The base simulation: Tessendorf FFT ocean

What Rare state (SIGGRAPH 2018):

- "The underlying ocean water simulation is an implementation of the FFT technique described
  in [Tessendorf 2001]" — i.e. Jerry Tessendorf, "Simulating Ocean Water" (SIGGRAPH 2001
  course notes). The talk's challenge was stylising/supplementing that FFT water to sit
  inside a stylised Unreal Engine 4 game rather than a photorealistic one.
  Sources: https://history.siggraph.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/2018-Talks-Ang_The-Technical-Art-of-Sea-of-Thieves.pdf
  and https://www.booksci.cn/literature_d302396.htm (abstract record, DOI 10.1145/3214745.3214820)

What the Tessendorf method is (general background, needed to implement the same base):

- Build a complex height spectrum `h0(k)` from an oceanographic spectrum — Phillips in the
  original paper, with directional spreading relative to wind and Gaussian random phases;
  JONSWAP/TMA are common modern substitutes that add fetch/peak-sharpening:
  https://www.gamedeveloper.com/programming/deep-water-animation-and-rendering and
  https://developer.nvidia.com/gpugems/gpugems/part-i-natural-effects/chapter-1-effective-water-simulation-physical-models
- Each frame, evolve the spectrum in time with the deep-water dispersion relation
  `ω(k) = sqrt(g·|k|)` and run a 2D inverse FFT to get a seamlessly tiling height field.
  Slopes/normals and a horizontal "choppiness" displacement come from companion spectra
  transformed the same way; the horizontal displacement sharpens crests into the choppy,
  non-sinusoidal profile real wind waves have.
  Sources: https://www.gamedeveloper.com/programming/deep-water-animation-and-rendering
  ("Given the properties of the FFT it can be seamlessly tiled over and over again") and
  https://developer.nvidia.com/gpugems/gpugems/part-i-natural-effects/chapter-1-effective-water-simulation-physical-models
- Folding/breaking detection: where the horizontal displacement folds the surface over
  itself, the Jacobian determinant of the displacement changes sign/goes small — the
  standard, cheap "this crest is breaking" signal used for foam (see §2.4):
  https://www.researchgate.net/publication/224211334_Ocean_wave_simulation_in_real-time_using_GPU
  and https://rtryan98.github.io/2025/10/04/ocean-rendering-part-1.html

### 2.3 Tiling and cascades (general FFT practice; Rare do not publish their tile layout)

- One FFT tile is periodic by construction, so a single tile repeated to the horizon shows
  obvious tiling, and one resolution cannot span both long swell and centimetre chop.
  The standard fix — used across production FFT oceans and in WebGPU demos — is cascades:
  several IFFTs (typically 3–4 tiles, e.g. 256² each) at different world sizes summed in
  the shader, ideally with tile sizes in non-integer/irrational ratios so their repetition
  periods rarely coincide; detail normal noise or a simpler wave model is blended in at
  distance where FFT tiling would otherwise show:
  https://www.intel.com/content/dam/develop/external/us/en/documents/bringing-anno-1800-to-laptop-gamers-around-the-world-the-long-road-from-10-to-30-fps.pdf
  ("blend them to simpler noise-generated waves to prevent the tiling of the FFT waves from
  becoming noticeable") and https://github.com/SamG-Coder/clearwater (WebGPU example: three
  256² cascades at 4.6 m / 37 m / 293 m sampled in world space) and
  https://jokin110.github.io/oceanSimulation.html (four cascades at 23 m / 101 m / 509 m / 2003 m).
- Treat any specific cascade sizes as illustrative starting points from those public
  implementations, not as Rare's numbers — the SIGGRAPH PDF does not disclose tile
  resolutions/sizes in the material recovered here.

### 2.4 Colour, subsurface look, and foam (Rare's stylisation layer)

From the SIGGRAPH 2018 PDF (quoted/paraphrased closely; see PDF link in §2.1):

- Water colour is a scattering approximation, not full PBR: a blend between a deep-water
  colour and a subsurface water colour, driven by view/light geometry and by a wave peak
  mask. "The wave peak mask is generated from the FFT choppiness vertex offsets." Visually:
  peaked/thinned parts of waves let light through (brighter, greener/turquoise), troughs
  stay deep. A summary of the recorded talk adds that the subsurface contribution uses a
  dot product of light and view vectors:
  https://www.summarize.tech/www.youtube.com/watch?v=y9BOz2dFZzs
- Crest foam: "Foam is generated at wave peaks using the method described in [Tessendorf]"
  (the Jacobian/folding criterion in §2.2), then combined with artist-authored foam textures.
  "Foam generation, dispersion and blending with the artist-authored textures is modified
  based on whether the water is calm, normal [or stormy]": calm water shows essentially
  only contact/intersection foam; storm water generates much more crest foam. Foam is
  dispersed over time (a feedback/blur-style dissipation in the talk's description) rather
  than switched on/off per frame, which is what makes it read as lingering churn.
- Intersection foam: foam "is also added around objects that intersect the water surface
  within a camera centered window using depth [information]" — i.e. a screen/camera-local
  depth comparison finds where hulls, rocks, and shorelines pierce the surface and deposits
  foam there, instead of simulating shoreline interaction in the FFT (which cannot know
  about geometry). This is the single most transferable trick for hulls and coasts.
- Stylisation principle reported from the project: start from the photorealistic FFT base
  and stylise on top (colour, foam shapes/textures), because simplifying the simulated
  fine detail itself looked wrong; the sea should still move like real water:
  https://mcvuk.com/development-news/rares-sea-of-thieves-started-with-photorealistic-water/
  and https://www.gamespot.com/articles/new-sea-of-thieves-video-shows-off-the-games-gorge/1100-6443348/
  (calm near shore vs large open-sea waves; whitecaps with wind).
- Secondary-source note (lower confidence): a survey article summarises Rare as also using
  small real-time GPU fluid simulations for "incidental" water such as deck splashes, on top
  of the FFT ocean: https://medium.com/@myas.drmax/creating-water-effects-principles-not-a-tutorial-782dd5188c52
  This was not verified against the SIGGRAPH PDF text recovered here; treat deck-water as a
  separate local effect in any case (see §2.6).

### 2.5 How ships interact with this kind of ocean

What is documented vs what must be designed:

- The SIGGRAPH material recovered here documents rendering-side interaction (intersection
  foam, §2.4), not Rare's networked buoyancy implementation. Player-facing behaviour in the
  shipped game — ships visibly ride, pitch, and roll with the waves — is widely observed but
  is not a substitute for an engineering source:
  https://spuf.org/2023/07/21/the-sea-in-sea-of-thieves/ (player-experience description only).
- The core engineering problem with a pure GPU FFT ocean is that gameplay/physics needs wave
  height (and ideally slope/velocity) on the CPU at hull sample points, while the FFT result
  lives in GPU textures. The three standard solutions, all applicable in WebGPU (this is
  general practice, not a claim about Rare):
  1. Deterministic hybrid: keep long, physics-relevant swell as an analytic sum (e.g.
     Gerstner/sine trains) evaluated identically in WGSL and in JS/TS for buoyancy, and let
     FFT cascades carry only shorter, lower-amplitude detail that a large hull largely
     ignores. Buoyancy error stays small; drawn surface ≈ sampled surface for big waves.
  2. CPU mirror: run a small CPU inverse FFT (e.g. 32²–64²) of the same spectrum/time step
     purely for physics sampling. Same inputs, so heights track the GPU surface at the
     wavelengths that move a ship.
  3. GPU readback: copy the displacement texture to a mappable buffer (`copyTextureToBuffer`
     + `mapAsync`) and sample it one or more frames later. Exact, but asynchronous and
     latency-prone in a browser; better for foam/spray triggers than for tight buoyancy.
     A third-party discussion notes Sea of Thieves is understood to use real-time FFT with
     async GPU readback for networked buoyancy — user-comment level evidence, not a Rare
     source, do not rely on it as fact:
     https://ruclips.net/video/rV6TJ7YDJY8/notgdc-2023-fft-ocean-flipbook-how-to-create-sample-one-using-blender-ue.html
- Gameplay-scale ship response (recommendation): sample height+normal at 4–8 hull points
  (bow/stern/port/starboard, plus midships for long hulls), fit a target plane/orientation,
  and critically damp the ship's pitch/roll/heave toward it with the big inertia of a
  container ship (compare §1.1: a large hull filters out short chop). Add separate,
  purely visual responses needing no physics sync: bow spray particles when the bow sample's
  relative vertical velocity and wave slope exceed a threshold; hull intersection foam via
  the depth method in §2.4; a fading wake in a world-anchored foam/history texture behind
  the stern. Deck wash/splashes belong to a small local particle/fluid effect, not the ocean.

### 2.6 Concrete WebGPU mapping (recommendations)

Pipeline per cascade, all in compute shaders (WGSL):

1. `h0` build (on parameter change only): Phillips or JONSWAP spectrum × Gaussian random
   phases → Hermitian-symmetric complex spectrum texture/buffer per cascade.
2. Spectrum evolution (per frame): apply `exp(±i·ω(k)·t)`, `ω = sqrt(g·|k|)`, producing
   height, x/z slope, and x/z choppy-displacement spectra. Pack pairs of real transforms
   into complex channels to halve the number of FFTs.
3. 2D inverse FFT (per frame): ping-pong Stockham or Cooley–Tukey butterfly passes
   (horizontal then vertical) over storage textures/buffers. WebGPU examples doing exactly
   this in WGSL: https://github.com/SamG-Coder/clearwater (Stockham butterfly passes,
   CUDA→WGSL) and https://vgpu.sh/examples ("Particles ocean … Stockham passes produce a
   displacement field") and the WebGL predecessor describing the same Stockham structure:
   https://relint.de/waves.html
4. Combine pass: assemble per-cascade displacement (xyz), normal/slope, Jacobian (for foam),
   and the choppiness-derived wave-peak mask into textures the render shader samples.
   Foam history: keep a persistent foam texture that accumulates Jacobian-generated foam,
   advects/blur-disperses it, and decays it each frame — the Rare "dispersion" behaviour
   (§2.4) — instead of thresholding the Jacobian fresh every frame.
5. Render: a camera-centred, world-space-sampled ocean mesh (clipmap-style rings or a
   projected grid) displaced by the summed cascades in the vertex shader, so tiles never
   move with the camera in texture space. Fragment: deep↔subsurface colour by peak mask
   and view/sun geometry (§2.4), Fresnel sky reflection, sun specular/glitter, foam texture
   modulated by foam history + crest foam, and depth-based softening at intersections.
   WebGPU caveat: the depth attachment being written cannot also be sampled in the same
   pass — render opaques first, copy/resolve depth to a sampleable texture, then draw water
   sampling that copy for intersection foam and shallow-water absorption. (Standard WebGPU
   constraint; plan the extra copy pass from the start.)
6. Distance treatment: fade the smallest cascade(s) and foam detail with distance/foam
   mip level, blend toward a cheap normal-noise sea at the horizon, and match horizon
   colour to the sky/fog — the reference videos (§1.4) are horizon-dominated, so the
   sea↔sky junction matters more than near-field micro-detail at the far plane.

Parameterisation that maps directly to the reference footage:

- Wind speed/direction + fetch (or spectrum peak period) as the master sea-state control;
  preset ladder mirroring Rare's calm/normal/storm foam regimes: calm harbour (§1.2,
  FFT amplitude near zero, intersection foam only), tropical trade-wind sea (§1.3),
  North Atlantic developing sea (§1.1, long fetch, high wind, heavy crest foam).
- Choppiness gain as the crest-sharpness control; foam thresholds tied to the Jacobian
  distribution of the current sea state, not fixed constants, so whitecaps appear
  progressively as in §1.1.
- Ship coupling via §2.5 option 1 or 2 for any vessel that must float believably; reserve
  readback (option 3) for effects. For a fixed-camera timelapse-style piece (§1.3),
  buoyancy can be faked entirely: animate camera/ship rig with slow filtered noise and let
  the FFT ocean be visual-only.

What not to copy blindly:

- Rare's non-PBR, hand-stylised colour/foam textures are art assets for a specific look;
  the transferable parts are the *signals* (peak mask, Jacobian, depth intersection), not
  the texture style. For a realistic cargo-ship piece, feed the same signals into
  photographic albedo/roughness foam and physical sky reflection instead.
- Unreal-specific material/render-target plumbing from the talk does not map to WebGPU;
  re-derive it as the compute-pass chain above.
- An FFT ocean alone has no shoreline behaviour (no shoaling, breaking on beaches, harbour
  damping). Harbours such as §1.2/§3 need an amplitude mask (calm zones), depth-tinted
  shallow colour, and intersection foam layered on top — plan those as separate systems.

How the legs read visually (synthesis tying §1.3 to the table):

- Waypoints 1–5: desert-coast straits opening into empty tropical ocean; clear, dark nights
  (the video's only early highlights are night sky). Longest "nothing happens" stretch.
- Waypoint 6–7: first port call and weather set piece (Colombo rain departure), then the
  busy but still ocean-scale rounding of Sri Lanka.
- Waypoints 8–10: land closes in and never fully leaves; lightning, haze, extreme traffic
  density, and the Singapore port cycle dominate.
- Waypoints 11–13: open sea again but populated — fishing-fleet lights at night, building
  trunk-route traffic, and a congested island-studded arrival as the climax.

Navigation caveats: eastbound/westbound TSS lanes are offset from these centreline points
(typically by a few km); monsoon-season routing around Socotra and in the South China Sea
varies with weather (see #4 source); port waypoints are harbour coordinates, while a ship's
lane passes pilot boarding areas several km offshore. Do not use this table for real
navigation.
